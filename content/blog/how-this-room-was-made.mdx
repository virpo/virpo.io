This website is a little grey handheld with my room inside it. Here's how I made it, mostly by asking for ten of everything.

The old one was fun: my head followed your cursor, there were little toys to learn Japanese, and sounds from the Japanese streets I love. But while showing you how fun I am, it got distractingly busy. :)

<ArticleImage src="/assets/blog/pocket-room/story/old-site.webp" alt="The old virpo.io: a big photo of Peter shouting, colourful toy buttons, a Tokyo clock and a grid of projects" title="The old one. Fun, and a lot." />

This time I wanted one proper toy, and a blog that stays readable, so it doesn't get in the way of the things I actually want to share.

My setup these days is Claude Code with Opus 5.5, paired with a ChatGPT subscription for adversarial reviews, but also images. I don't paste prompts anywhere: Claude calls Codex itself. More on that later.

## The machine gun

Making something beautiful on the first try is luck. Let's cheat luck.

When unsure, shoot a machine gun with 10 options. What also works is not describing the answer or how to get there, just what the answer should pass: what qualities it needs, what's important to me in how it looks.

```prompt
My website is interesting, but busy. I like the Japanese toys, but there's no calm, no 3D, nothing extra. Research what top designers do that most people haven't seen, and give me 10 concepts to pick from.

It has to work as:
- a blog: a shared post reads top to bottom, the fun stays in a sidebar
- a landing page with my 3 newest posts and a way to see more
- a list of my projects
- a home for my Japanese toys, a click away

I'll share posts on Hacker News, so it needs credibility, but keep it playful. On surprise it should be 10 out of 10. Check whether ChatGPT can render the visual assets.
```

<ArticleImage src="/assets/blog/pocket-room/story/concepts-1-3x3.webp" alt="Nine of the ten concept paintings, with 06 (an isometric house) and 08 (a little handheld device) circled in red pencil" title="Round one. I chose 06 and 08." />

I picked on instinct: what could make a good website, and what caught my eye first. I love isometric drawings (06), and 08 is inspired by Teenage Engineering.

> **Technique: the machine gun**
> When unsure, ask for ten. Describe what the answer has to pass, not the answer. Recognising the good one is much easier than imagining it.

I thought the two could be merged. Fitting a whole house into a pocket machine would be far too much detail, but the agent knows that too. I only asked for them to be merged in a way that works visually and still passes the first prompt. I often let it go wild, and even ask it to surprise me.

## The first build was horrific

I let it build 03, frontend only, to see what it is even capable of. The results were absolutely horrific.

<ArticleImage src="/assets/blog/pocket-room/story/first-build.webp" alt="The first build: a 3D vinyl diorama of a house with numbered dots, next to a list of posts" />

<ArticleImage src="/assets/blog/pocket-room/story/first-builds-rest.webp" alt="Six more early builds: the same diorama at night and dusk, and three versions of a device next to a column of text" title="And six more, none of them the picture I picked." />

It wanted to rebuild the house in code, but as you can notice, it can't draw. So I pointed it at ChatGPT's image generation for every manga-style picture, and let it call Codex by itself. I didn't overspecify much: what I wanted to see, plus one trick for cutting things out.

```prompt
Build out 03 for real, and make it look like no other website. You can't draw, but ChatGPT can: call its image generation through codex exec, with the mockup attached as the reference, so every picture shares one style. Paint anything that moves or opens on flat #FF00FF, so you can cut it out. Look at every image before you keep it, and paint it again when it's off.

Make the character look like me, make the trains move, and make it obvious on hover what can be clicked. It has to work on phones too. Multiple agents, multiple tries, pick the best one.
```

> **Technique: Claude calls ChatGPT for images**
> The Codex CLI puts ChatGPT's image tool in your terminal, so Claude Code can run it like any other command, with reference images attached:
>
> ```sh
> codex exec -i room.png -i crop.png - < prompt.txt
> ```
>
> Ask for each picture to be saved to an exact path, and have it look at every result before it keeps one.

<ArticleImage src="/assets/blog/pocket-room/story/three-tries.webp" alt="Three new attempts at the handheld, each with a painted room on its screen, the third circled in red pencil" />

The third one worked for me: a room with enough space in it. Some details were off, sure, but that's something to work with. From here I started pointing at what to fix, while still letting it rebuild big parts. I didn't want to commit to one way of doing the 3D if there was a better one.

## 3D, but not plastic

The buttons weren't really 3D. I asked for real depth, but stressed that it must not end up like the plastic 3D house from before: it had to keep the visual, material and tactile quality of the concept we picked. Then I pointed at every detail that put me off.

## A tiny world on pink paper

The world inside is clickable, moves a little, and you can play with it. The trick is that everything is painted on a flat pink background, so a script can cut it out.

<ArticleImage src="/assets/blog/pocket-room/story/pink-plate.webp" alt="The painted room with its windows painted flat magenta" title="The whole room is one painting. The pink is where the window view goes." />

<ImagePair
  left={{ src: "/assets/blog/pocket-room/story/pink-props.webp", alt: "Notebooks, a mug and a cat painted on magenta" }}
  right={{ src: "/assets/blog/pocket-room/story/pink-peter.webp", alt: "Six poses of mini Peter painted on magenta" }}
/>

> **Technique: paint on #FF00FF, then cut**
> Nothing in a cosy painting is that pink, so turning magenta into transparency gives you clean sprites. Every object you can hover is cut out of the one painting, so everything lines up.

There were small problems. Clicking an item showed a blurry, ugly cut-out of it. So it took the cut-out as the reference and painted it again, bigger and without the background.

<ArticleImage src="/assets/blog/pocket-room/story/cards.webp" alt="The arcade and the tiny TV, each as a blurry cut-out from the room next to the same object painted again on its own" title="Cut out, then painted again: the arcade and the TV." />

Knowing I'd most likely stick with this website, I also asked it to write down how it makes these pictures, prompts included, into the repo. If I put something new on my desk long after this conversation is gone, it will know where to pick up, and the new thing will match this world.

```prompt
Store how you made every image here: the prompts, the reference photos, the scripts and the steps. Assume the next agent has none of this conversation. If I say "the cat on the bed should become a new project", it should be able to paint it, cut it out and wire it up so it matches the rest of the room. Also write down which looks I picked and which I turned down, so nobody tries them again.
```

> **Technique: make the agent write its own recipe**
> Every prompt lives in a text file next to the picture it made and the photo it started from, and an `AGENTS.md` points the next agent at it, along with the looks I already turned down.

## Hands

AI and hands. It took us about six tries. Every time I thought "now I've explained it properly, it will just know". Not even looking with its own eyes helped. We got there with cut-outs of exactly what had to disappear and what had to stay.

## Projects, ten ways

Lastly, I wanted to show my projects at the bottom. At first it just made a list:

<ArticleImage src="/assets/blog/pocket-room/story/project-list.webp" alt="A plain list of projects: titles, one line each, and the room object each one belongs to" title="Correct, and nobody would look at it." />

I wanted it visual, but without stealing attention from the main act at the top. We ended at a colourless grid with a thumbnail per project. It took ten ideas, two iterations and hand-picking what did and didn't work. Including the reveal, which I first wanted to look like a drop of ink landing on paper. It was just too distracting.

<ArticleImage src="/assets/blog/pocket-room/story/youtldr-9.webp" alt="Nine versions of the YouTLDR thumbnail, from a screenshot of the site to a pencil and colour painting of a YouTube card turning into No." title="Versions of one thumbnail, from the real site to the one that stuck." />

<ArticleLoop src="/assets/blog/pocket-room/story/loop-reveal.mp4" poster="/assets/blog/pocket-room/story/loop-reveal.webp" label="A project card in pencil fading into colour when the pointer reaches it" title="Pencil at rest, colour when you point at it. A plain fade won." />

I didn't know the right answer to a lot of things, like how much manga the pictures should have. So I asked for five intensities and picked one.

<ArticleImage src="/assets/blog/pocket-room/story/manga-5.webp" alt="A photo of a Stream Deck at five strengths, from the original photo to full manga, with a slider underneath from weak in green to strong in red, stopped at the fourth" title="From weak to strong. I stopped at medium." />

You see the theme. When I imagine something, I don't have to be sure. I can ask for everything and pick.

Just don't forget to kill some ideas, or you'll never ship. :)
